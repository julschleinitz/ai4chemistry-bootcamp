Start here

Before any code

Fill in this sentence first. It is the first line of your plan template — if it needs the word "and," you have two projects; pick one.

I want to predict · rank · generate · explain ___________ for ___________, so that I can ___________, instead of ___________.

The plan, in order

Five headings, one page

  1. A question worth answering. Name a result that would prove you wrong. If you can't find one, the question isn't sharp enough yet. Would you be interested in the answer if it came out the other way?

  2. The data you actually have. Source, deduplicated sample count, the label and its error, whether negative results are recorded, and the cost of one new data point.

  3. What the model is for. Not "predict the property well" — name the job: filter, ranker, interpolator, generator, or explainer. The job determines the metric.

  4. Enough data, or not? If yes: design the split, and say why holding that out mimics how the model will really be used — the split is the scientific claim. If no: design the acquisition, and name the first 24 experiments specifically, not just a strategy.

  5. What done looks like. The baseline you have to beat, what will exist by Friday, and where you're stuck — the most useful section for your reviewer.

The job determines the right metric — and who actually uses the output.

Can existing data answer your question?

Yes → the split is the scientific claim — choose it before you see a single score, write it in the brief, and do not change it afterward.

Watch for

Six ways this goes wrong

×

Duplicates across the split — same compound, two names, two salts, two tautomers. Deduplicate on InChIKey before splitting.

×

Tuning on the test set — every look costs you a little of it. Keep a validation set, touch the test set once.

×

Descriptors that leak — batch, date, plate position, sample ID. If MW is your top SHAP feature, ask why.

×

Extrapolation dressed as prediction — report where test points sit relative to training. Outside the hull, it's a guess with a decimal point.

×

Metric mismatch — optimising RMSE when the job needed enrichment.

×

No baseline — still, somehow, the most common one.

Artrith, N. et al. Best practices in machine learning for chemistry. Nat. Chem. 2021, 13, 505–508. doi.org/10.1038/s41557-021-00716-z — two pages, free, read it tonight.

Thursday

Peer review, in seven questions

You'll trade briefs with a peer and review the framing, not the chemistry — assume the author knows their system far better than you do. Be specific and be kind, in that order, and say what's good first. Use the peer-review template, save as Lastname_Firstname_plan_review.docx, and send it to jul@caltech.edu.

  1. Say it back. Without looking at their sentence, write their project in one sentence of your own. If you can't, say exactly where the plan stopped making sense.

  2. Question, or topic? Name a result that would prove them wrong. Would you care about the answer if it came out the other way?

  3. Is the data enough? What didn't they tell you that you'd need before believing a result? Anything likely duplicated, correlated, or measured under different conditions?

  4. Does the evaluation match the intended use? Does their held-out set resemble how the model would actually be used? If they're acquiring data, are the first 24 experiments the ones that teach the most?

  5. Is the baseline honest? Is it beatable, and hard enough that beating it means something?

  6. The most useful next step. What could they do in the next three hours that would tell them the most — including that the project doesn't work?

  7. What you would steal. One thing from their plan you'll take back to your own project.

Grading, loosely

What done looks like

Tier 1

A brief and a baseline — sharp question, honest data inventory, baseline measured.

Tier 2

+ one model on your own split, and a number you're willing to defend.

Tier 3

+ the diagnosis — why it worked, or why it didn't. Rare in three afternoons.

All three are a pass. You are not practising how to fit a model — you are practising how to decide what is worth fitting, and that is the only part of this week nobody can hand you.
